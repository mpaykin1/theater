The scientific foundations of my method

Sound, movement, intonation and words — four ways to experience, express and make sense of emotion.

Читать по-русски →Individual work →

Our mental state is connected not only to thinking, but also to movement, breathing, voice, bodily sensations and social interaction.

In my practice I bring together movement, sound, intonation and words. This is an original creative and educational approach. Its individual elements have been studied in neuroscience, psychophysiology and psychotherapy research.

01 / Movement

Regulating emotion through the body

Movement engages the brain's motor systems and proprioception: our awareness of the body's position and motion. It can help people notice bodily responses, explore different ways of acting and express experiences that are difficult to put into words.

A 2026 meta-analysis combined 19 randomized controlled trials involving 1,375 participants. Dance and movement interventions were associated with reduced depressive symptoms, but the results varied considerably between studies. These findings concern movement interventions, not a direct evaluation of my method.

Study: Xing, 2026 · PubMed ↗

02 / Sound

Breathing, voice and physiological arousal

Singing, sustaining a sound or vocalizing long vowels changes how we control the out-breath and use our vocal apparatus. We hear our own voice and feel its vibration. When vocalization is paired with comfortable, slow breathing, it may accompany changes in autonomic regulation.

A meta-analysis of 31 studies involving 1,133 participants found that slow-paced breathing, on average, improved certain measures of heart-rate variability and slightly reduced heart rate. These results concern breathing specifically; they do not establish that every vocal exercise produces the same effect.

A Cochrane review also found benefits of music therapy as an addition to standard depression care. Music therapy, however, is not equivalent to my vocal practice.

Shao et al., 2024 ↗ · Cochrane, 2017 ↗

03 / Intonation

How the voice communicates emotion

Pitch, speed, volume, pauses and timbre help people convey feelings and perceive another person's emotional state. By changing intonation, I invite people to explore different ways of expressing the same experience.

A meta-analysis of 66 studies found a positive association between emotional expression in psychotherapy and therapeutic outcomes. This does not prove that changing intonation alone is a treatment: content, context and the relationship between people matter.

Peluso & Freund, 2018 · PubMed ↗

04 / Words

Naming what I feel

Statements such as “I am angry”, “I am afraid” and “I want to protect myself” give an emotional experience a name. Researchers call this process affect labeling.

In a neuroimaging study by Matthew Lieberman and colleagues, labeling emotions was associated with a reduced amygdala response to negative images. In another study involving spider fear, participants who labeled their emotions showed a reduced physiological fear response at follow-up. However, self-reported fear did not differ significantly between groups.

Lieberman et al., 2007 ↗ · Kircanski et al., 2012 ↗

05 / Integration

Why I combine all four channels

My method combines movement for embodied experience; sound for breathing and vocal activity; intonation for emotional expression; and words for awareness and meaning. Together, these offer different ways to explore a real-life situation through action, sound and speech.

The approach shares some techniques with multimodal expressive practices and psychodrama. A 2025 systematic review examined 27 psychodrama studies and reported promising findings while emphasizing methodological limitations, differences between interventions and the need for stronger research.

Maya et al., 2025 · PMC ↗

Scientific description of my method

Multimodal psychophysiological regulation through movement-based, vocal, prosodic and verbal expression

My aim is to help a person do more than intellectually understand an emotion: to explore it, express it and experiment with regulating it through the body, voice and language.

What the evidence does — and does not — establish

Research supports potential benefits of certain movement, breathing, expressive and verbal interventions. But there are no direct clinical studies establishing the effectiveness of this exact combination or its superiority to other methods. My practice is an original creative and educational format; it is not a medical service and does not replace medical, psychiatric or psychotherapeutic care.
